# Flammability limits
*Too little fuel or too much, and a spark does nothing.*

## At a glance
| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Methane | 5-15 % in air |
| Propane | 2.1-9.5 % |
| Hydrogen | 4-75 % |
| Acetylene | 2.5-≈100 % |
| Carbon monoxide | 12.5-74 % |
| Gasoline vapour | 1.4-7.6 % |
| Ethanol vapour | 3.3-19 % |

## Why there are limits
A flame survives only if the heat released by the burning layer is enough to heat the next layer to reaction temperature. Below the **lower flammability limit (LFL)** there is too little fuel: the flame can't heat the excess air. Above the **upper limit (UFL)** there is too little oxygen. Between them is the explosive range.

## What shifts them
- **Hotter mixtures** have wider limits (less heat needed to reach ignition).
- **Higher pressure** mostly raises the upper limit.
- **Inert gas** narrows the range until it closes: below about 12 % oxygen methane can't burn at all (limiting oxygen concentration).
- Mixtures of fuels follow **Le Chatelier's rule**: 1/LFL = Σ yᵢ / LFLᵢ.

A tank 'full' of gasoline vapour is above its UFL and won't explode; an 'empty' tank with a little vapour left is the dangerous one.

## In Ignis
- Fuel clouds that are too lean or too rich won't light; rich clouds burn only where air mixes in.
- Burnt products and nitrogen dilute a mixture until it stops burning.

## Try this
Spray a quick puff of fuel and light it. Then spray for five seconds in the same place and try again at the centre of the cloud.
