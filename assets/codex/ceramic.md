# Ceramic
*Shrugs off 2800 K flames, but cracks if you heat or cool it too fast.*

## At a glance
| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Density | 3950 kg/m³ (alumina); 144 kg/m³ (Shuttle LI-900 silica tile) |
| Melting point | 2072 °C alumina; 2715 °C zirconia |
| Thermal conductivity | ≈30 W/(m·K) dense alumina; ≈0.05 W/(m·K) LI-900 tile |
| Thermal expansion | ≈8 ppm/K (alumina) |
| Compressive / tensile strength | ≈2500 / 250 MPa (alumina) |

## In the real world
Technical ceramics (oxides, carbides, nitrides) are bonded so strongly that they survive temperatures where every metal has melted. Their weakness is brittleness: they cannot deform to relieve stress, so a sudden temperature gradient makes the hot side expand against the cold side until a crack runs through. Porous silica tiles insulated the Space Shuttle so well you could hold one by the edges seconds after it left a 1260 °C oven.

**Uses:** Kiln linings, spark plug insulators, heat shields, turbine thermal barrier coatings, rocket nozzle inserts.

**Hazards:** Thermal shock: splash cold water on a red-hot ceramic and it shatters.

## In Ignis
- Softens at **≈2100 K**, melts at **≈2850 K**: the most heat-resistant wall.
- Excellent insulator (0.12× steel conductivity): the gas side glows while the far side stays cool.
- **Thermal shock**: if the temperature difference across neighbouring cells exceeds about **2.2 units (≈660 K)** it cracks. Heat it gently.
- Weak under pressure (strength **1.2**): back it with a metal shell.
